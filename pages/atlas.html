An Atlas of Mexican Federal Law: Laws, Regulations, Guidelines and Treaties

315 laws, 863 regulations, 125 guidelines and 1,373 treaties, placed by where their excerpts’ closest texts live

Every federal law, regulation and guideline, and every international treaty, that the Supreme Court publishes in consolidated form is one point on this map. Two instruments sit close together when the texts nearest to their excerpts live in the same places, so that the organic laws of the public universities, for instance, land beside one another whatever words they happen to use, and the treaties gather in a region of their own.

Where the instruments come from

The texts come from the legislative database of the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation (legislacion.scjn.gob.mx/consulta), which keeps for every instrument a table of its reforms and serves the consolidated text of the instrument as it stood after each one. The project reads the database through the Court’s own search service, which gives every instrument a stable identifier. The Diario Oficial de la Federación remains the official source of legal text, and the consolidated versions of the Court are an editorial product.

The 315 laws, which include the codes and the Constitution, are published as the scjn-leyes corpus, and the Federal Laws page describes it in full. The list of federal laws was seeded from the catalogue of federal legislation that the Cámara de Diputados publishes (LeyesBiblio, 316 instruments), which gave each law its name and its abbreviation. Each law was then searched by its name in the Court’s database and resolved by the identifier the Court gives it, and never by the rank of the search, because the Court’s search can return a wrong document for a name. Its whole reform table was crawled, which gives one dated text for every reform, 3,707 texts in all.

The Court’s search has no way of listing everything, so the 1,087 regulations (scjn-reglamentos) and the 163 guidelines (scjn-lineamientos) were found through a set of phrase searches. These did not use the search form of the site but the search service behind it, which accepts the scope and the category of the instruments as part of each request even where the form does not offer them as choices, and which answers every instrument together with its category, as the listing of the site shows. Each phrase was sent to the service for the federal scope and for the category REGLAMENTO or LINEAMIENTOS, the service answered in pages, and every page was read to the end, so that the union of the results of the phrases is the list of candidates. The phrases for the regulations were reglamento, de, a, la, y, para and el, and those for the guidelines were lineamientos, lineamiento, de, a, la, y, para and el. Besides the word of the category, the short common words are there so that an instrument of the category whose title does not contain that word is still reached. To these were added the instruments that the Court files under another category but whose own reform table has a row of that category, which were looked for among the hits of one phrase, reglamento for the regulations and lineamientos for the guidelines, and which rescued 6 regulations and 4 guidelines. Because the Court reissues a regulation under a new identifier instead of reforming it, these two corpora are keyed by that identifier and not by name. For 5 regulations and 37 guidelines the Court serves no text at all, so they belong to the corpora but cannot appear on the map.

The treaties were found in a different way, because the Court does not file a treaty as a federal instrument but gives it a scope of its own, Tratados Internacionales. A treaty is therefore any instrument of that scope, whatever its category, since the category of a treaty is as likely to be a convention, an agreement or a protocol as a treaty in the strict sense, and a list of categories would have left hundreds of them out. The scope was read in pages through the same search service, with a union of the phrases tratado, convenio, convencion, arreglo, carta, declaracion, pacto, protocolo, acuerdo, de, a, la, y, para and el, which serve only to page through the scope and do not decide whether an instrument belongs to it. This found 1,455 treaties (scjn-tratados), and for 82 of them the Court serves no text, so that 1,373 appear on the map.

The map uses the newest consolidated text of every instrument, one per instrument. Since regulations are reissued, several regulations or guidelines share one name, and the map keeps only the most recently issued of them, names being compared after folding accents, case and whitespace and only within a collection, so that laws are never grouped, and neither are treaties, since no two of the 1,373 share a name. This leaves 315 laws, 863 regulations, 125 guidelines and 1,373 treaties, which are 2,676 instruments once 220 have been dropped (219 regulations and 1 guideline). The current text of each of them is then divided into excerpts, as the section on what is compared describes.

How to read the map

A point

A point is one law, regulation, guideline or treaty. Its area is proportional to its counted excerpts, that is, to the number of articles and other pieces of text into which it is divided and which are compared with those of other instruments (its headings and transitory articles are not). Its colour is its collection, blue for laws, green for regulations, magenta for guidelines and yellow for treaties.

Distance

Two instruments sit together when the closest texts of their excerpts live in the same places, and not when they use the same words. The map is therefore a picture of where federal law borrows from itself and repeats itself.

The numbers

Each excerpt, a block of text cut from an instrument whatever its role, was matched to the single closest text outside its own instrument. A match shared by m instruments gives 1/m to each of them, so that every excerpt that counts adds up to one.

Searching for an instrument or clicking a point fills the panel with the five instruments to which its excerpts are closest, together with the number of its counted excerpts that went to each, and with the five instruments whose excerpts point at it most often. Lines on the map join the selected instrument to its five closest instruments and are numbered as in the panel, and a name in the panel selects that instrument, so that the relations can be followed from one instrument to the next. The map can be zoomed by scrolling or pinching and moved by dragging, and the selection is cleared by pressing Escape or by clicking an empty spot. The collection buttons under the map show one collection at a time.

Each weight under Closest instruments is itself a button. It opens a table of the excerpts behind that number, each beside the closest text the other instrument has, both in full, together with how similar the two are and how much the match adds to the weight, which is 1, or 1/m when the matching text is shared by m instruments.

What is compared

An excerpt is a piece of an instrument’s text that is set beside those of the other instruments, and it is an article, whatever its length, the preamble, the closing text, a heading or a block of loose text. The text that represents an article is its own text as it stands in the consolidated law, with its number label and nothing added, so that neither the name of the law nor the heading of the chapter in which the article sits becomes part of what is compared. Article 1 of the LEY que crea la Universidad Autónoma Chapingo is therefore represented by

ARTICULO 1o.- Se crea la Universidad Autónoma Chapingo como organismo descentralizado del Estado, con personalidad jurídica, patrimonio propio y sede de gobierno en Chapingo, Estado de México.

and not by the heading of the chapter above it, CAPITULO I De su Naturaleza, Objetivos y Medios, which is left out.

Exactly this text is what the three methods receive. Each Qwen3-Embedding model reads it whole and turns it into a single vector, of 1,024 numbers for the 0.6B and of 2,560 for the 4B, and the similarity of two excerpts is the cosine of their vectors. BM25 reads the same text as the lowercase words it contains. No instruction, title or law name is added for any of the three, and an article longer than the window of a model, as happens to a handful of very long articles, is cut at that window.

Two kinds of excerpt are not compared at all, neither as the excerpt being matched nor as the text it is matched to. The first is the heading, such as the chapter heading above, and the second is the transitory article, which repeats the standard wording of a decree, such as the first of the Chapingo law, PRIMERO.- Esta Ley entrará en vigor a los quince días de la fecha de su publicación en el “Diario Oficial” de la Federación. The preamble and the closing text, on the other hand, are compared. A third kind is matched but not counted, and it is a text that several instruments share word for word. Article 56 of the CÓDIGO Civil Federal is of this kind, since its text, which reduces to the label ARTICULO 56.- of an article with no body, is exactly the text of article 56 of the LEY Federal de Responsabilidades de los Servidores Públicos, of the LEY General de Población and of the LEY Orgánica de la Administración Pública Federal, so that the match says nothing about any one of them.

The Chapingo law accordingly has 28 excerpts, which are 16 numbered articles, 5 transitory articles, 5 headings (its four chapters and the heading of the transitory articles), the preamble and the closing text. The 5 headings and the 5 transitory articles are not compared, and the remaining 18 count, as the example below shows.

On the map the law sits among the organic laws of Antonio Narro, the UNAM, the UAM and the IPN. The relation is directed, since 21 excerpts of other instruments have their closest text in Chapingo’s law, 7 of them in Antonio Narro’s and 7 in the UAM’s.

The Constitution has 1,328 excerpts, but only its 136 articles and its preamble are compared, so that 137 count. Its closest instrument is the LEY General de Instituciones y Procedimientos Electorales, at 14.0. The table behind that weight lists 14 excerpts, every one a whole 1, among them articles 41, 51 to 56, 58 to 60 and 63 on elections and Congress. The closest of them, article 96, is matched to article 494 of that law at a similarity of 0.86, and the weakest, article 58, to its article 10 at 0.67. The next closest instruments are the LEY Orgánica del Poder Judicial de la Federación at 9.0 and the LEY Orgánica del Congreso General de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos at 8.0, and a treaty, the Convención Americana sobre Derechos Humanos, is among the five closest at 5.0.

Treaties mostly resemble one another. For 1,284 of the 1,373 treaties the closest instrument is another treaty, and only 8 laws and 5 regulations have a treaty as their closest instrument.

The neighbourhood size

The map is drawn four times from the same data. Each layout is a two-dimensional UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) embedding of every instrument’s row of weights (McInnes et al. 2018). UMAP builds a graph that joins each point to its nearest neighbours and then lays that graph out in the plane so that neighbours stay close. The neighbourhood size is the number of neighbours in that graph, so that small values preserve tight local groups, such as the universities above, while large values preserve the overall shape, such as how the laws, the regulations, the guidelines and the treaties sit relative to one another. Switching it changes the layout and never the relations listed in the panel.

The similarity

Two excerpts are close when the method that compares them says so, and the map can be drawn from any of three methods. Two of them are Qwen3-Embedding models (Zhang et al. 2025), which turn each excerpt into a list of numbers. The smaller, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (the 0.6B), is the one on which the map opens and the one that the examples above describe, and the larger, Qwen3-Embedding-4B (the 4B), is a model of the same family. The third, BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza 2009), is a lexical baseline and not a model at all. It ranks texts by the words they share, weighting each word by how rare it is among the excerpts being compared, so that it knows nothing beyond the corpus itself and does not match an excerpt with a differently worded one that means the same. Its scores are not on a scale from 0 to 1, and the table behind each weight therefore calls them scores and shows one decimal.

Choosing another method is not a change of view like the neighbourhood size. A different method finds different closest texts, so the instruments listed in the panel, the weights and the lines all change, and the layout is drawn again from them. The selected instrument stays selected and the neighbourhood size stays where it was. The map of the 4B or of BM25 is fetched the first time it is chosen, and the table behind each weight is then read for that method.

Checking the map against known links

The three methods agree on the closest instrument for most instruments, but agreement is not correctness, and a method that all three share could be wrong in the same way. The corpus itself offers a way to check, because an instrument often says which other instrument it develops, applies or amends. A regulation exists to develop one law and usually says so, a law may be reglamentaria of an article of the Constitution, and a treaty may be the protocol of an earlier one. Such a statement is a strong link between two instruments that no similarity method was used to find, and it can be used as a gold standard against which the three data sets are scored.

How the strong links are built

A strong link goes from one instrument to another, and nothing restricts what the two are. Every one of the 2,676 instruments on the map can be the source of a link and every one can be its target, whichever collection each belongs to, under a single rule, which is that an instrument is never linked to itself. When two instruments carry exactly the same name, as the two LEY de Amnistía do, or as a regulation of the Senate does with the law it was filed beside, the name is the instrument’s own and it is not read as a mention of the other.

The links come from three signals, each read from the text of the source. The first, A, is the name of the instrument. When the name of a regulation contains the name of a law, as in the REGLAMENTO DE LA LEY FEDERAL DE CORREDURÍA PÚBLICA, the law is the Ley Federal de Correduría Pública. The same reading links a law to the instrument it regulates, the LEY de Amparo, Reglamentaria de los artículos 103 y 107 de la Constitución Política de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos to the Constitution, and a treaty to the one it implements, the AVISO POR EL QUE SE DA A CONOCER EL ESQUEMA DE CERTIFICACION QUE LA REPUBLICA SOCIALISTA DE VIETNAM APLICARA EN EL MARCO DEL TRATADO INTEGRAL Y PROGRESISTA DE ASOCIACION TRANSPACIFICO to that treaty. A treaty is often cited by a short form of its name, so a treaty name of five words or more is also read without its trailing qualifier and up to its first comma, and the name of an instrument that merely extends another with more words is never read as a link to it. Signal A links 161 instruments: 130 regulations, 19 treaties, 10 laws and two guidelines. The second, B, is the sentence that states the purpose of the instrument, usually its first article, which often names what it regulates. Article 1 of the REGLAMENTO DE LA LEY DE AGUAS NACIONALES reads “El presente ordenamiento tiene por objeto reglamentar la Ley de Aguas Nacionales”, which confirms what its name already said. Article 1 of the REGLAMENTO DE SISTEMAS DE COMERCIALIZACIÓN MEDIANTE LA INTEGRACIÓN DE GRUPOS DE CONSUMIDORES reads “El presente ordenamiento tiene por objeto regular los sistemas de comercialización mediante la integración de grupos de consumidores, a que se refiere el artículo 63 de la Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor”, so that its law is found although its name never mentions it. Signal B links 170 instruments, and the two signals together link 251 instruments through 275 links.

The links run in many directions, though most of them go from a regulation to a law. By the collection of the source and of the target, there are 215 links from regulations to laws, 23 from treaties to treaties, 12 from laws to laws, nine from guidelines to laws, six from regulations to regulations, five from regulations to treaties, three from guidelines to regulations and two from laws to treaties. The 21 treaties that are the source of a link all point to another treaty, as the ACUERDO PARA LA APLICACION DE SALVAGUARDIAS EN RELACION CON EL TRATADO PARA LA PROSCRIPCION DE LAS ARMAS NUCLEARES EN LA AMERICA LATINA Y EL TRATADO SOBRE LA NO PROLIFERACION DE LAS ARMAS NUCLEARES does.

A name is not always enough. For 62 instruments the name points to an instrument that cannot be resolved among the 2,676 of the corpus, as with the REGLAMENTO DE LA LEY FEDERAL DE TURISMO, whose law has been abrogated, and these instruments are left out of the gold standard instead of being guessed.

The third signal, C, works on single excerpts instead of whole instruments. An excerpt that cites “artículo 38 Bis de la Ley General del Equilibrio Ecológico y la Protección al Ambiente” names the instrument it is about, and the same three data sets can be asked where that instrument ranks for that one excerpt. The corpus holds 7,066 such citations, in 5,218 distinct excerpts of 1,925 instruments that cite 316 different instruments, once the 258 citations of an instrument to itself have been set aside. Of those excerpts, 4,497 cite exactly one instrument. An excerpt that cites two or more instruments is counted but not scored, because it has no single right answer, and 29 more are removed because their text is word for word the text of several instruments. The 4,468 excerpts that remain are scored, and 1,934 of them cite the Constitution, which every kind of instrument cites. Laws, regulations, guidelines and treaties all appear among the instruments that cite, and a treaty cites a law far more often than another treaty, with 863 pairs of an excerpt and a cited law against 37 of a cited treaty.

What is measured

At the level of the instrument, each source is scored against its gold instrument, or its gold instruments when it has several. The panel orders the other instruments by the weight they receive from the source, which is the number printed under Closest instruments. The measure closest is a gold instrument counts the sources whose gold instrument is the one at the top of that order, and the measure a gold instrument among the five closest counts those for which a gold instrument is among the first five. The mean reciprocal rank averages, over all the sources scored, the reciprocal rank of the best-placed gold instrument, which is 1 divided by its position in the order, so that it is 1 when the instrument is first, 1/2 when it is second and 0 when it receives no weight at all.

At the level of the excerpt, each citing excerpt is compared with the texts of the 2,675 instruments other than its own. Every one of those instruments is scored by the similarity of its closest text to the excerpt, and the instruments are ordered by that score. The rank of the cited instrument is its position in that order, an instrument whose score only equals the cited instrument’s not being placed ahead of it. The measure cited instrument first is the share of excerpts for which that rank is 1, and the measures among the five closest and among the ten closest are the shares for which it is at most 5 and at most 10. The mean reciprocal rank is the average of 1 divided by the rank, and the median rank is the middle value of the rank over all the excerpts, out of 2,675.

The scores behind these tables are computed by the same three methods and under the same rules as the map, so that the tables measure the map itself.

Two methods are compared measure by measure, and a single test is used for each measure. For a measure answered yes or no for every instrument or excerpt, which are closest is a gold instrument and a gold instrument among the five closest at the level of the instrument, and cited instrument first, among the five closest and among the ten closest at the level of the excerpt, McNemar’s exact test is applied to the cases on which the two methods disagree, and the difference is real when its p-value is below 0.05. For the mean reciprocal rank and the median rank, the difference between the two methods is given with the 95 % interval of a paired bootstrap, and it is real when the interval does not contain zero. This rule was fixed before the results were seen, and when it finds no real difference the two methods are tied.

At this level the methods are no longer tied. The 4B is better than the 0.6B on all five measures and better than BM25 on four of them, the exception being the share of excerpts whose cited instrument is first, on which the two cannot be told apart, and the 0.6B and BM25 are tied on every measure. The differences are small, two to three points of recall, but they are not noise. The absolute level is low because an excerpt that cites an article of a law is usually about something other than the rest of that law, and the Constitution, which is cited by 1,934 of the excerpts, drags it down further. The order of the three is the same without the Constitution.

The level depends on who cites. The cited instrument is among the five closest for 35 %, 41 % and 38 % of the 1,528 excerpts of laws under the 0.6B, the 4B and BM25, for 26 %, 28 % and 26 % of the 1,901 excerpts of regulations, and for 32 %, 35 % and 28 % of the 185 excerpts of guidelines. For the 854 excerpts of treaties it is 2.7 %, 2.3 % and 1.9 %, with a median rank above 1,400 in every method. A treaty that cites an article of a Mexican law mentions it in passing, inside an agreement about something else, so that the law’s own text is probably far from the excerpt, and none of the three methods bridges that gap.

The reading

On the question the map answers, which is what an instrument is closest to, the three methods are indistinguishable at finding the instrument that another one develops, and a lexical baseline that learns nothing is as good as either embedding model. The 4B ranks a cited instrument higher for a single excerpt, and this is the one measured reason for switching to it. BM25 is not beaten everywhere. Among the ten most cited instruments it is the best on the Código Fiscal de la Federación, where the cited instrument is among the five closest for 79.7 % of the 172 excerpts against 44.2 % for the 4B, and the lowest of the three on the Constitution, with 5.2 % of 1,934 excerpts against 8.8 % for the 4B. These checks look at the instrument and not at the article, so they say nothing about whether the right article of that instrument ranks first.

Data. The texts are the four consolidated corpora this project publishes from the Supreme Court (SCJN), namely the laws (scjn-leyes), the regulations (scjn-reglamentos) the guidelines (scjn-lineamientos) and the treaties (scjn-tratados). They are divided into excerpts and embedded with Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, the default of the map, and with Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-4B, to which the similarity control switches, as it does to BM25, a lexical baseline computed from the same excerpts. Of the 203,324 excerpts, 31,172 are headings and 26,182 are transitory articles, neither of which is compared, and 1,947 are word-for-word texts shared by several instruments, which are matched but not counted, so that 144,023 count. The map is a UMAP projection with the cosine metric of each instrument’s matches. The Diario Oficial de la Federación remains the official source of legal text. Map data generated on 12 October 2026.
